opencast Reference: Network desk
Ref. 12Internal app for Opencast's team, web

Filling a dial, one yes at a time.

Network desk is Opencast's own tool for building a market: seeing every channel on the dial and what fills it, finding local creators and asking permission, turning a yes into a running station, and managing the city streams and catalog programming that fill the rest. Claimable stations are the core of it. PEG listings, the public-domain catalog and reserved call signs sit on the same board, because they all answer the same question: what's on this dial tonight?

Same tokens as everything else, with an "Internal" mark in the header. It's the Inland Empire market, Saturday, September 26.

Permission before air

Either the creator said yes, or their work is already published under a licence that allows it, like CC BY. Nothing is copied or aired on anything less, and the yes or the licence is kept as the rights record.

One board for the dial

Independent stations, claimable stations, city streams, the catalog station and reserved call signs, all on one map of the market.

Recipes, not hand-built logs

A claimable station starts from a template schedule filled from the creator's work and the catalog, so the team can run many without hand-scheduling each.

Their money, held in escrow

What a claimable station earns is held in an escrow contract under the station's ID. The contract can only pay the creator, or the creator fund if it's never claimed.

Every number in the market at a glance. Gaps are visible as open slots, which is how the team decides what the market needs next.

02The creator pipeline

From found to claimed, one row each.

Every local creator the team is talking to, where they are, where their work lives, and the next thing to do. Most go found, asked, said yes, setting up, on air, then claimed or declined. Creators whose work is already published under a licence that allows commercial use, like CC BY, skip asking: their station can go on air with attribution while they're invited to claim it.

Sorted by the next action

Overdue follow-ups and new yeses first, finished rows last. The pipeline is a to-do list, not a funnel chart.

Where the work lives

The source matters twice: it's where the work is imported from, and it's the account the creator connects later to prove it's them when they claim.

One reminder, then stop

A creator who hasn't answered gets one reminder after a week. After that the row is marked "No answer" and the team doesn't ask again.

Declines are respected, and kept

A creator who said no is kept on the list so nobody asks twice.

"Already licensed" is checked per work

The licence has to allow commercial use, because stations carry spots. CC BY does; the non-commercial variants don't. Each work's licence and link are saved, and the credit airs as the licence requires.

Works are chosen, not assumed

The team ticks which works to include and leaves out anything that might belong to someone else, like a sponsor edit. Only ticked works are covered by the yes.

The message is short on purpose

A personal note, two sentences of what Opencast would do, and one link. The details live on the permission page, where they can be read properly.

Show them their station

The message includes tonight's schedule as it would be, using their own titles. Seeing it gets more yeses than describing it. It's built from titles and lengths only; no files are copied before the yes.

Sent where they already are

A message on the platform where their work lives, plus email if there is one. Never a cold call, never a follow-up beyond the one reminder.

Ready before the yes

The channel, call sign and recipe are prepared from titles and lengths while the ask is out. Files are only copied once the yes arrives, so a yes can be on air within the hour.

Why the call sign is careful

Call signs are permanent once a station signs on. The team suggests one from the creator's own name, and the creator is told it's theirs to keep.

Permission is the rights record

Every imported item's rights are "Permission from the owner", pointing at the yes. That's what makes these imports different from the station-local link imports in the library.

Recipes keep it light

A recipe by category fills the day from the creator's work, the catalog and one carried local program, with sensible repeat limits. One team member can look after several stations.

A local anchor in every station

Each recipe carries one local public-affairs program, like Council Watch, so every new station helps the civic side of the dial too.

05City streams and the catalog

What fills the dial without a creator.

City and county meeting streams are listed, not restreamed: Network desk keeps each source, syncs its meeting calendar into listings, and checks that its terms allow embedding. The catalog station is Opencast's own, filled from the catalog. Both sit on the board with everything else.

Embedding is checked, not assumed

A source is only listed when its terms allow embedding. "Terms unclear" means someone asks the district first.

Listings come from their calendar

Where a city publishes an agenda calendar, meetings appear in the guide automatically with their real titles and times.

The catalog station steps aside

Opencast's own station holds a channel until a local station wants it, then moves. The dial should fill up with local stations, not with Opencast.

Other markets' streams

Whether a city stream from outside a market can be listed in it, for example a county meeting that covers two markets, isn't decided.

After saying yes.

No account to say yes

The yes is recorded against the link, with a timestamp and the exact list of works, and a copy is emailed. An account only matters when they claim.

Stop is always one tap

The same link stops everything later, so saying yes never feels like a trap.

Claiming early

A creator who'd rather run it themselves can claim straight away and go to the normal sign-on flow, with the recipe already filled in.

The permission wording

This page is a licence in plain words. It should be reviewed by a lawyer before the first creator sees it, as noted when claimable stations first came up.

07Held earnings

Every claimable station's money, held in escrow.

Claimable stations don't get wallets. Their earnings, after the same fees and pool share as any station, are paid into one escrow contract each week, held under the station's ID. The contract has only three ways out: to the verified creator when they claim, to them if they ask to stop, or to the creator fund if it's never claimed. It has no way to pay Opencast.

One contract, a balance per station

One escrow contract holds every claimable station's earnings, each under its own station ID, so each creator's money is exactly what their station earned. Wallets only exist for people who have signed in.

Fees come off first, like everyone

Opencast's share and the pool apply to a claimable station the same as to every other station. What's left is the creator's.

"It can't be"

Not a promise: the contract has no function that pays Opencast, and anyone can read it. The only thing Opencast controls is who is verified as the creator.

Verification is the trust point

Claims are approved by a multi-signature key, not one person, and every approved claim waits 72 hours in public before it pays out, so a wrong claim can be challenged.

Where the contract lives

Opencast's own contract, or part of the Clear protocol alongside its encumbrance. Which chain, and the 3-year unclaimed period, are defaults to confirm.
