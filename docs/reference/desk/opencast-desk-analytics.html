Ref. 12dNetwork desk, analytics

Every station, added up and taken apart.

The Network desk's analytics page. The whole network first: how many were tuned in, where, on what, and to which stations. Then any station, program, market or day on its own. Every number comes from data Opencast already collects, or names the small addition it needs. Part 08 lists the source of each one.

Same tokens and Network desk shell as Ref. 12a to 12c. Tuesday, October 6, 2026, looking at the last 7 days: Tuesday, September 29, to Monday, October 5.

Sessions, not people

Opencast counts tuned-in sessions and never knows who's watching. The page says "sessions" and "tuned in", and never "viewers" for something it can't count.

Network, then any slice

One set of filters (dates, market, band) runs through every tab. Any station, program or market opens to the same views, narrowed.

Counted apart is shown apart

Relays and other apps never go into the network total. They have their own lines, so nobody bills on them by mistake.

Every number has a source

Each figure traces to a table and a rule, and the CSV matches the page. Estimates are labelled as estimates.

Stayed to the end

Of the sessions tuned in at a program's first minute, the share still there at its last minute, averaged over the station's airings. It's the clearest single sign of whether programming holds people.

"Not for me" per 1,000 hours

Votes divided by hours watched, so a big station and a small one compare fairly. Only votes from sessions that watched at least 2 minutes count.

Under the minimum

FLDR never had 20 tuned in at once this week. The station sees "Not enough viewers yet". The desk sees hours and presets but not the per-airing numbers, which would be too few to mean anything.

External stations

RDLS plays its own stream, so there's nothing to earn and no dead air of ours. What it does have is time down: minutes its source was unreachable while someone was tuned in.

The chart, airings and surfaces match BEAT's Audience page in master control to the number. The desk adds flow, cost to run and the comparison with the network.

Breaks are shaded

Each break in the night is a band. Most dips line up with them, which is what Programs and breaks (05) measures across the network.

Flow, from session minutes

A session's minutes in a row show where it was before and after. "Started here" means the session began on BEAT; "Stopped" means it ended there. Session rows go after 30 days, so the counts are added up every night and kept.

Cost to run is an estimate

Storage, relay hours, live hours and preparing are measured. Their price comes from a cost table in Settings, so the dollars are labelled "estimated". The margin line is what makes a free plan's real cost visible.

The grid shows the shape of the week

One square per hour per day, darker for more tuned in. Prime time on Friday and Saturday stands out at a glance, and so do dead hours worth programming differently. Hover any square for its number and last week's.

Sessions under a minute aren't counted

A session counts once its player has advanced media time. Bots are filtered before anything is counted, and the filtered share is shown with its reasons in Health (07).

Moving around the dial

Which changes happen most tells you which stations share an audience. That's useful for carriage offers ("REEL's viewers already come to BEAT") and for where to put a new station's number.

How people tuned in

Opencast doesn't record whether a session started from a swipe, the guide, the keypad, search or a shared link. Knowing that would show which part of the viewer app finds people stations. It's one optional field on the heartbeat (see 08).

By position and first element
Breaks that open with a bumper keep 4 points more than breaks that open straight into a spot. 61% of breaks this week opened with a bumper.

A program across stations

A maker's program counts every airing, its own and its carriers', the same way master control shows makers their numbers. Carriers' airings join the totals once two of them reach the minimum audience together.

Still watching, not total audience

The curve follows the people there at the start, so a program that gains viewers partway doesn't look like it held everyone. Tune-ins partway show in average tuned in, in the table.

Break hold is the ad number

It's what a spot buyer really pays for: people still there when their spot airs. Measured per break, by length, position and what opens it.

Counting breaks needs finer timing

Minute samples are too coarse for a 30-second break. Break hold is worked out from session heartbeats around each break's start and end, and kept per break (see 08).

Earned, from the ledger

Every line is a sum of ledger entries, so the CSV lists the entries behind each number. Carriage moves money between stations, so it's shown as volume and never added to the network's earnings.

Per 1,000 hours

Ad money per 1,000 hours watched compares stations of any size. A low number on a big station means its breaks are open, not sold. That's a spot market lead.

Opencast's share is still open

The shares rule isn't set, so Opencast's share shows "Not set yet" in Settings' words, never a guessed $0. It fills in on the date the rule takes effect.

The honest line

Pay-as-you-go charges against the estimated cost to run. It's negative this week, which is expected before launch, and it's the number to watch as free plans grow.

Off air isn't a problem

Planned off air hours are their own grey part of the bar and never count against a station. Dead-air fill and the slate are the mistakes: time nobody scheduled, or something that wasn't ready.

Incidents explain themselves

Each one says what happened, how long, how many were tuned in, and what playout did instead. That's the line the desk sends a station when it reaches out.

Relay restarts are routine

Planned restarts timed to breaks (YouTube's 12 hours, Twitch's 48) aren't incidents. A relay that dropped outside a break is.

Not collected, on purpose

People. Opencast counts sessions: one person on a phone and a TV is two sessions, and the same person tomorrow is a new one. There's no "unique viewers" across days, no age, gender or other demographics, no address and no location finer than the market. Signed-in watch history stays the person's own and never feeds these pages. The page never uses "viewers" for a number it can't count; it says "tuned in", "sessions" or "hours".

Rules the pages follow

The minimum audienceBelow 20 tuned in at once (the watch_data.minimum_audience rule), a station sees "Not enough viewers yet". The desk sees hours, presets and sessions, but not per-airing numbers, and the station row is marked "Under the minimum".
Who sees whatAdmins see everything. Market leads see their market only, with the filter fixed. Rights reviewers don't see analytics.
Freshness"Today" reads minute samples live, every minute. Every other span reads the nightly totals, updated at 6:00 am market time, and says so beside Export CSV.
ComparisonAlways the span just before, of the same length. "Today" compares with the same weekday last week, to the same minute.
ExportsEvery tab exports its tables as CSV, with the same columns and filters as the page.
