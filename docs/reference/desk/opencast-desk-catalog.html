Opencast's own shelf, and proof it's free to air.

The Catalog page in Network desk: the programs Opencast packages and offers to every station at no cost. Each series is built from individual films or recordings, and each one carries its own rights record, because "public domain" is decided item by item, not by collection. This is where the team adds items, checks their rights twice, builds them into episodes, and sees where they air.

Rights per item, not per collection

A box of 1930s cartoons can be half public domain and half not. Every film or recording has its own basis, evidence and checker.

Two people say yes

An item is offered only after two team members have checked it. Every station carrying it stands on that check.

Originals, not someone's restoration

A modern restoration can carry its own new copyright. Items come from original prints and archives, and the source says so.

Free to stations, credited to a sponsor

Catalog series cost stations nothing and leave them the break time, apart from one sponsor credit an hour.

"Mixed, per short"

In the US in 2026, works published in 1930 or earlier are public domain. Later ones from before 1964 are only public domain if their copyright wasn't renewed. A series can mix both, so the basis column says so and the item list says which.

Old radio is the hard one

Rights in 1940s radio dramas vary show by show: scripts, recordings and music can have different owners. The mystery hour only offers episodes that have passed, and says how many are still in review.

Stations see the same basis

The rights basis here is the line stations see in the syndication market under "Rights", so a station knows what it's standing on before it carries anything.

Items, then episodes

An episode is a sequence of items with bumpers, prepared once like any program. Rebuilding an episode re-prepares only what changed.

A failed item comes out everywhere

When a check fails after the fact, the item is pulled from every episode, those episodes are rebuilt, and carrying stations get the new version on their next airing. It's the same principle as a rights claim, started by Opencast.

Characters and trademarks

A public-domain film can still show a character or logo someone holds a trademark in. Catalog titles, descriptions and artwork never use them to promote the series.

03Adding an item: the rights check

A checklist with evidence, then a second person.

Adding an item starts from its source. The checklist asks for what makes it free to air, each answer with evidence attached: the source, publication year and notice, the renewal search, the soundtrack, and trademarks. Then a second team member reviews it before it can go into any episode.

Evidence is kept with the item for as long as it's in the catalog, so any question later can be answered from the record.

The rules live in configuration

The public-domain cut-off year moves forward every January 1, and the rules differ for sound recordings and outside the US. The checklist reads them from settings rather than hard-coding them.

Evidence or it doesn't pass

Every "yes" has a file or a record attached. A checklist with no evidence can't be sent for the second check.

US works first

The catalog starts with US works for US markets. Offering items in other countries would need that country's rules checked too.

A lawyer's once-over

The checklist and the renewal-search method should be reviewed once by a copyright lawyer before the catalog launches. After that, the two-person check carries it.
